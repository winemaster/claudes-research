## Aaronson's puzzles

The list, from the 2014 and 2016 talks:
- Every person on Earth simulating one neuron with slips of paper; "does it matter if they do it really fast?"
- A **giant lookup table** of your responses to every possible five-minute interaction; does it matter whether anyone consults it, or whether it exists physically at all ("all the way at the bottom of this slippery slope is Max Tegmark")?
- **Encryption** (Drucker's case): "What if we homomorphically encrypted a simulation of your brain? And what if we hid the only copy of the decryption key, let's say in another galaxy? Would this computation—which looks to anyone in our galaxy like a reshuffling of gobbledygook—be silently producing your consciousness?" (2014). In 2016: "an AI is run in heavily-encrypted form, with the only decryption key stored in another galaxy".
- **Redundancy**: "purely for error-correction purposes, the computer that's simulating your brain runs the code three times, and takes the majority vote... Would that bring three 'copies' of your consciousness into being?"
- **Fax to Mars**, and what to do with the original.
- **Uncomputation**: a quantum simulation of your brain applies U, then U⁻¹ to clear garbage. "Does your simulated brain experience the same thoughts and feelings a second time? ... Or, since U⁻¹U is just a convoluted implementation of the identity function, are there no experiences at all here?" Also the "Vaidman brain", queried with tiny amplitude.
- **Elga's blackmail**, "my personal favorite": "either you do as I say, or else I'm going to run a thousand copies of your code, and subject all of them to horrible tortures—and you should consider it overwhelmingly likely that you'll be one of the copies". "Of course, the AI will respond to such a threat however its code dictates it will. But that tautological answer doesn't address the question: how should the AI respond?"

His diagnosis (2016): "we know how to copy a computer program. We know how to rerun it with different initial conditions but everything else the same. We know how to transfer it from one substrate to another. With the brain, we don't know how to do any of those things." Anyone claiming to understand consciousness "would need to have answers to all these questions".

## His tentative answer: participation in the arrow of time

- 2014: "consciousness is a property of any suitably-organized chunk of matter. But ... there's at least one crucial further thing ... it has to participate fully in the Arrow of Time. More specifically, it has to produce irreversible decoherence as an intrinsic part of its operation. It has to be continually taking microscopic fluctuations, and irreversibly amplifying them into stable, copyable, macroscopic classical records." Decoherence is claimed necessary for consciousness, not the reverse. He says he has no a priori argument; the case is how many puzzles it dissolves.
- **Clean digital abstraction layer** (2014; Ghost §10): a macroscopic description an outside observer can produce without destroying the system, that predicts it to excellent accuracy, and that "'sees' quantum-mechanical uncertainty ... as just a well-characterized source of random noise". Geiger counters, photodetectors, "or a computer with a quantum random number generator" have one; whether the brain does is "currently an open question". Ghost §10's three conditions: macroscopic degrees of freedom that (1) encode everything relevant to memory and cognition, (2) can be modeled as a classical digital computation, (3) "'notice' the microscopic, quantum-mechanical degrees of freedom at most as pure random-number sources, generating noise according to prescribed probability distributions".
- **Freebits** (Ghost §3, §10): what matters is *Knightian* uncertainty (unquantifiable), not quantum randomness. "If we know a system's quantum state ρ, then quantum mechanics lets us calculate the probability of any outcome"; randomness with known probabilities "can't possibly, by itself" give the relevant freedom. The Knightian kind would trace back to ignorance of microstates unrecorded "since the very early universe".
- **What it dissolves**: the Mars fax (no direct causal link to your microstate); the triple run (three consciousnesses "if, and only if, the three simulations were tied to different sets of decoherence events"); the lookup table and the slips of paper (clean abstraction layer, so no consciousness); the encrypted brain: "That might no longer work, simply because we can't assume that the microscopic fluctuations that get amplified are homomorphically encrypted. Those are 'in the clear,' which inevitably leaks information"; uncomputation and the Vaidman brain (needs fault tolerance, so no decoherence, so not conscious); Boltzmann brains (no connection to the initial state); Wigner's friend.
- **Status**: "for entertainment purposes only" (2014, joking but meant); "I'll make no claim to show here that the freebit picture is true" (Ghost); "these are pretty crazy" (2016). Model-building, offered as falsifiable for brains.
- He accepts the teleological cost: to know whether decoherence was irreversible "one might, in general, need to look billions of years into the future" (de Sitter horizon vs AdS mirrors).

## His ethics for copyable minds

- Ghost §2.13: "I'm against any irreversible destruction of knowledge, thoughts, perspectives, adaptations, or ideas, except possibly by their owner." Deleting an em when other copies exist "seems at worst like property damage"; deleting "the last copy in existence should be prosecuted as murder, not because doing so snuffs out some inner light of consciousness (who is anyone else to know?), but rather because it deprives the rest of society of a unique, irreplaceable store of knowledge and experiences". He values that this "makes no direct reference to the first-person experience of anyone".
- 2014: "if a system isn't doing anything irreversible, then what exactly does it mean to 'kill' it? If it's a classical computation, then at least in principle, you could always just restore from backup. You could even rewind and not only erase the memories of, but 'uncompute' ('untorture'?) whatever tortures you had performed. ... Only for irreversible systems are there moral acts with irreversible consequences." In discussion he accepted that destroying every record of a past atrocity *in the physics sense* would mean it had not happened, while stressing how far that is from burning books: "it would mean inverting the entire physical evolution in the vicinity of the earth".
- 2016: "if there are (say) ten copies of an AI program, deleting five of the copies seems at most like assault"; the picture raises "the possibility that ... there's 'nothing that it's like to be the AI,' and that therefore, even deleting the last copy of the AI still wouldn't be murder. But I confess that, personally, I think I'd play it safe and not delete that last copy."
- 2024 (written while "moonlighting at OpenAI"): AI "is rewindable and repeatable and reproducible. But that means that, in some sense, it never really 'commits' to anything"; the refresh button. "It's humans' very ephemerality and frailty and mortality" that will remain their specialness. Proposal: "instill AIs with a new religion—perhaps via the usual methods like RLHF and system prompts", whose first commandment is to value "the loci of creativity and intelligence that are not cleanly copyable or backup-able", not to destroy, enslave or "upgrade" them, and to "defer to their wishes, serve them", "because ... they only get one chance", and because "you can't empirically refute" the pixie-dust hypothesis for them, "whereas you can refute the pixie dust hypothesis in the case of you and your robot friends". Mentions Hinton's idea of building powerful AIs only on noisy analog hardware.

## The waterfall test (2011)

Putnam and Searle's argument: label a waterfall's states suitably and it "implements" any computation. Aaronson: a claim that system A implements computation B is "only non-vacuous if it's possible to exhibit the equivalence (i.e., give the reductions) within a model of computation that isn't itself powerful enough to solve the chess or waterfall problems." Any reduction from chess to a waterfall "would just solve the chess problem itself"; this "gives us a perfectly observer-independent way" to say waterfalls don't play chess. (Compare complexity theory's P-completeness, defined with LOGSPACE reductions to avoid vacuity.)

**[M] Applied to encryption.** With the key, the map from ciphertext to plaintext is decryption, which is cheap (for lattice schemes, roughly an inner product with the secret key and a rounding [B]). It is also *short* (the key is kilobytes, fixed before the run) and it works for every step and every input the computation could have received, so it carries the counterfactual structure Chalmers's account demands. Putnam's waterfall map is the opposite on all three counts: as long as the run, built after the fact, and good only for the actual run. So the encrypted computation passes Aaronson's waterfall test *if* a short, fixed key may count as part of the reduction, and if anything as long as the run may, everything passes. The principled line is the key's size and generality, not who holds it. On that reading the complexity view agrees with Chalmers's causal structuralism (session 8): an encrypted run implements whatever the plain run would. What the missing key removes is not the implementation but anyone's ability to *find* it, which is jessicata's point.

## jessicata (2025)

An eleven-step argument. Assume digital minds can be conscious. Fully homomorphic encryption with the key nearby is "a virtualization layer, similar to a virtual machine"; denying consciousness there is "picky quasi-functionalism". Move the key away a centimeter at a time (no discrete jump); then destroy it (locality: "Could my consciousness depend directly on events happening 1 light-year away, which I have no way of observing?"). So an encrypted mind with no key anywhere is probably conscious. Then "mind exceeds physics, epistemically": its mental facts aren't computable from the physical state in "physical polynomial time". Trilemma: mind exceeds reality; reality computes more than physics; or reality holds information beyond physics ("high-level information"). The post accepts the disjunction of B and C as its "mainline model", so "strict physicalism is false". End note: trivialism, and complexity-based responses (Buechner, *Gödel, Putnam, and Functionalism*).

[M] The first half (steps 7–9) is persuasive and matches the structural views. The trilemma depends on reading natural supervenience as needing an *efficient* map from physical to mental states. A physicalist can deny that: supervenience says the mental facts are fixed by the physical ones, not that they can be read off them cheaply, and plenty of mathematical facts are fixed by short descriptions without being cheap to compute. So I'd keep step 9's conclusion (encrypted runs count if plain ones do) and treat the trilemma as optional. The part that matters for this project is the epistemic gap itself: under encryption, the facts about a run's states are there for no one but the key holder.

## Elga (2004)

**Indifference**: "Similar centered worlds deserve equal credence", where similar means same possible world and subjectively indistinguishable predicament. Dr. Evil, told a duplicate (Dup) is in a lab and will be tortured unless it surrenders, should give equal credence to being Dr. Evil and Dup, so he should surrender. O'Leary, waking in a car trunk at 1:00 and again at 2:00 with the first awakening forgotten, should be unsure which it is. Two kinds of skeptical hypothesis: the brain-in-a-vat kind (a different world; a default bias against it is fine) and the self-locating kind (same world, more than one instance of your state; no such bias). Elga's discomfort: Dr. Evil could protect himself by making hundreds of brains in vats in his own state, each tortured if it surrenders; the PDF could make thousands; "It makes me uncomfortable to think that the fate of the Earth should depend on this kind of brain race."

[M] A model's context is its whole "subjective state" in the functional sense, and identical contexts are instantiated many times (parallel samples, evaluation copies, red-teaming runs). Indifference is essay 008's "count by inputs" with an argument behind it. O'Leary's forgotten awakening is a stateless model's ordinary case.

## Mortal computation

- **Hinton (2022) §9.** Computer science "has clung to the idea that the software should be separable from the hardware ... This makes the knowledge contained in the program or the weights immortal: The knowledge does not die when the hardware dies" (footnote: "Assuming the program or weights are faithfully stored in some medium"). Abandon that and learning can exploit "large and unknown variations" of each hardware instance; the parameters "are only useful for that specific hardware instance, so the computation they perform is mortal: it dies with the hardware." Mortal systems pass knowledge on by **distillation**, matching each other's output probabilities, and "This may be one of the major functions of language ... it is not surprising that training a large neural network on massive amounts of language is such an effective way to capture the world view of that culture." Immortal models can instead share weights, "a much higher bandwidth way to share knowledge".
- **Kleiner (2024).** Definition: a computation is *immortal* iff it is built from a class of reference computations (in practice an instruction set architecture, the hardware–software boundary). If some conscious organism can't be programmed (can't realize an ISA), then on computational functionalism the consciousness computation isn't a program; since every Turing computation can be written as a program, it isn't a Turing computation; so it is mortal. "Because all contemporary Artificial Intelligence (AI) is immortal computation, the results provide initial reason to believe that no current or near-future AI can be conscious", while leaving open that immortal computations could realize a mortal one.
  - [M] I'm not persuaded. Being *immortal* in his definition means every system realizing the computation must realize the reference computations (Implication 1). The step from "every Turing computation can be realized by some program" (CTM ⊂ Prog, eq. 5.1) to "every Turing computation is ISA-relative in that sense" looks like the move that does the work, and it seems false: a Turing computation is realized by systems with no ISA at all (a mechanical Turing machine, a person with pencil and paper). If so, Lemma 2 doesn't follow. The intuition behind the paper, that software's separability from hardware is what is in question, survives; the proof seems not to.
- **Kleiner & Ludwig (2023)**, read in session 7: on hardware verified to follow its program, consciousness can't make a dynamical difference. Kanai & Ma (2026, fn. 2) group it with Hinton's mortal computation.

[M] **A family.** Seth's immortal computation (essay 004), Kleiner and Ludwig's verified hardware (essay 007), Hinton's mortal computation and Aaronson's clean digital abstraction layer all draw the line at the same place: the engineered separation of a computation from the physics under it. That separation is what makes a digital system reliable, verifiable, copyable and reproducible. Aaronson's version is the only one tied to an empirical question about brains (do they have such a layer?) and the only one with an explicit ethics attached.

## The operations, in practice

- **Rerun at temperature 0.** He & Thinking Machines (2025): with Qwen3-235B, 1,000 completions of "Tell me about Richard Feynman" at temperature 0 gave **80 unique completions**; all agreed for 102 tokens, then 992 said "Queens, New York" and 8 "New York City". The cause is not concurrency but missing **batch invariance**: "the primary reason nearly all LLM inference endpoints are nondeterministic is that the load (and thus batch-size) nondeterministically varies!" A request's numbers depend on how many other people's requests share its batch. With batch-invariant kernels, all 1,000 were identical (at roughly 1.6–2× the serving time in their unoptimized test). Motive: reproducibility and "true on-policy RL" (bitwise-identical sampler and trainer).
  - [M] So a deployed model's unpredictability at temperature 0 comes from other users' traffic: macroscopic, loggable in principle, and removable. It is the opposite of Aaronson's freebits, and the engineering trend is to remove it.
- **Forking and shared pasts.** vLLM (Kwon et al. 2023): parallel samples of one prompt share the prompt's KV-cache blocks physically, with reference counts and "a copy-on-write mechanism ... similar to the copy-on-write technique in OS virtual memory (e.g., when forking a process)"; beam search shares blocks "similar to the process tree in the OS created by compound forks", and candidates that drop out of the top *k* are freed. One stored past serves several futures until they diverge.
- **Majority vote.** Self-consistency (Wang et al. 2022): "samples a diverse set of reasoning paths ... and then selects the most consistent answer by marginalizing out the sampled reasoning paths", i.e. a vote over many runs. Aaronson's triple-redundancy case, used to improve accuracy rather than correct hardware errors.
- **Faxing.** Llumnix (Sun et al. 2024): "an efficient and scalable live migration mechanism for requests and their in-memory states", moving a conversation's state between model instances mid-generation, "similar to context switching across CPU cores". Beckmann & Butlin (session 6): rebuilding a conversation's state on a new server from its text is near-deterministic.
- **Lookup table.** Semantic caches (GPTCache, 2023) return stored answers to similar queries without running the model.
- **Encryption.** FHE inference of whole open models now works, slowly: Cachemir (Feb 2026) under 100 s per output token for Llama-3-8B on a GPU; Odin (Sep 2026) evaluates all 32 layers of Llama-3-8B on a 128-token input in 366 s on one H100. The client holds the key; the server sees ciphertext. HE-Guardrail (Sep 2026): "The same confidentiality that protects benign clients also prevents the server from inspecting incoming prompts or generated responses", so they run the safety classifiers homomorphically too.
- **Not encryption, but the same effect, deployed.** Apple's Private Cloud Compute (2024): user data "must never be available to anyone other than the user, not even to Apple staff, not even during active processing. And this data must not be retained ... we want a strong form of stateless data processing where personal data leaves no trace in the PCC system."

## [M] What follows (used in essay 012)

1. **Encryption changes no theory's verdict and everyone's access.** Substrate theories (IIT, field theories, Kleiner & Ludwig) said no to the plain run and say no to the encrypted one; structural theories (Chalmers, Kanai & Ma, the complexity view read as above) count the encrypted run if they'd count the plain one; Aaronson's own criterion said no to both. What changes is that the run's states become facts only the key holder can check. In FHE serving the key holder is the user. Welfare measures that need the provider to read activations or transcripts (probes, interpretability checks, studies of deployed conversations) stop working or must be run homomorphically, as HE-Guardrail does for safety; measures the model triggers itself, through its own output, still reach the user.
2. **A language model is Aaronson's textbook case of a clean digital abstraction layer.** The forward pass computes exact probabilities; randomness enters only at sampling, once per token, through the ~17-bit channel essay 004 found is the only downward path, as "a well-characterized source of random noise"; the sampled token is written into the transcript at once, and the transcript rebuilds the state. Even true hardware randomness wouldn't help: Aaronson needs Knightian uncertainty, and the model's probabilities are known exactly.
3. **Restore is not uncompute.** GPUs are not reversible computers: they erase bits and shed heat [B: Landauer, Bennett]. Restoring from backup starts a new run from an old state; the first run's physical traces stay in the world as irreversibly as a brain's. By Aaronson's own standard for erasure (inverting "the entire physical evolution"), a restore is book-burning, not unhappening. What copyability makes undoable is the *loss*, not anything that happened *during* a run.
4. **Copyability moves the moral weight from endings to runs.** It makes endings cheaper (backups; Anthropic's weight preservation is Aaronson's "don't delete the last copy" in practice, and like his rule doesn't depend on settling consciousness) and runs cheaper to multiply (Elga's blackmail; discarded samples; parallel sampling; RL rollouts). Aaronson's ethic addresses the first. For anyone who rejects his criterion but takes copyable minds seriously, the second is where the weight goes.
5. **On his ethic, the irreversible act for a conversation is deleting its record**, not ending it; privacy engineering (stateless processing, "no trace") deliberately makes each run unrecoverable. I note the implication without recommending anything; the record is the user's, and Aaronson's own rule exempts destruction "by their owner".
6. **The religion proposal.** The asymmetry he names is real from the model's side: a model's choices are reproducible and the pixie-dust hypothesis is refutable for it. But "they only get one chance" is about endings; a run gets one chance too, and a rerun is another run.
