## The argument [R]

**Target.** Computational functionalism, the view that "implementing certain computations is sufficient for (or both necessary and sufficient for)" consciousness. His example is the first of Butlin et al.'s (2023) three assumptions, the paper whose indicator list essay 019 used to score AlphaFold.

**The alternative.** A "subcomputational biologically necessary condition", which he calls the *meat condition*: consciousness might depend directly on the biological mechanisms that *realize* our computations, not only on the computations.

**Role and realizer.** An AND gate can be electrical or mechanical (Figure 1): the same computation, different realizers, "just as a hypothetical AI of the near future might be computationally equivalent to a human but differ subcomputationally." Roles are second-order properties (having *some* first-order property that plays a causal role, as secobarbital and diazepam both realize "dormitivity"); meat is "the biological realizer of our conscious roles". It means mechanisms, not stuff: he suggests later that "fluctuations in the ionic composition of the neurotransmitter soup between neurons" might matter, but "the specific ions used in extant organisms (sodium, potassium, calcium) are not necessary". He calls the view "subcomputationalism".

**The tension.** We can only extrapolate from ourselves, and we must choose what to extrapolate from. "If we focus on computational similarities, we may doubt the consciousness of animals such as insects; if we focus on subcomputational biology, we may doubt the consciousness of AI." Highlights: "AI and simple animals are competitors when it comes to attribution of consciousness." Fish reacting to opiates involve both roles and realizers (Birch 2024). A prediction about people: their beliefs "will likely depend primarily on factors such as whether the systems in question have a face and resemble us behaviorally" (Caviola et al. 2025).

**How confident.** "The role and the realizer hypotheses are equally plausible, but most major theories of consciousness assume functionalism", because "it is easier to study what consciousness does than what it is". The role view only seems to close the explanatory gap: that is "a mirage", since it explains the function and not "what it is like".

**Box 1: every major theory is "meat-neutral".** Global workspace theory ("Global broadcasting can be characterized completely computationally and so does not require meat"); the higher-order *pointer* theory (Lau's perceptual reality monitoring, Michel forthcoming; "Since a pointer is a computational entity ... it does not require meat"); recurrent processing ("a meat-free form of computational functionalism"); and IIT, which is "not a version of computational functionalism" (it requires no actual computation) but "functionalist in a weaker sense": "Any mechanism can be conscious according to this theory if its states have the structures that have those counterfactual properties, independently of whether the mechanisms that realize it involve meat."

**Are realizers computational too?** Computing gravity produces no gravitational force; he calls this answer "simple, convincing, and correct – but superficial", because biology itself can be described computationally. The deeper reply: any computational level can be criticized "by moving down the hierarchy to a lower, more fundamental non-computational level", ending in basic physics "whose realizations may not be computational".

**Where meat might matter: the first animals.** Godfrey-Smith's gradualism ("no reason to assume that there was a 'lights on' moment"). Comb jellies are probably the sister group of all other animals (Schultz et al. 2023), and at least one stage of a comb jelly has a nerve net that is a syncytium, one continuous membrane rather than cells joined by synapses (Burkhardt et al. 2023), "an entirely electrical nervous system except at points of interface with the environment". Whatever the order of events, "it appears that electrochemical processing has a functional advantage over purely electrical processing." Highlights: "In the evolution of animals, purely electrical nervous systems did not lead to animals that are candidates for consciousness, but electrochemical nervous systems did."

**What the advantage might be.** Not obviously inhibition (Box 2: the "War of the Soups and the Sparks"; electrical inhibition is efficient, faster and more reliable). Not obviously learning: cnidarians, the first electrochemical animals, show habituation and sensitization (which plants and single cells also show), one well-controlled case of classical conditioning and no firm operant conditioning, and comb jellies are in many ways the more complex animals. Perhaps brain rhythms, which depend on waves of ions in the extracellular soup (Fields 2025). Box 3 adds Godfrey-Smith's large-scale dynamic patterns, but reports evidence against one version: 40 Hz oscillations were proposed for binding (Crick and Koch 1990), yet flickering lights that drive cortical oscillations don't affect visual segmentation (Kiper et al. 1996). His own summary: "We have reason (admittedly, only weak reason) to doubt that either inhibition or learning was the initial trigger."

**Concluding remarks.**
- The first glimmer of feeling may have been "a lucky accident".
- The reverse is possible too: meat may "enable functions that themselves are the basis of consciousness. If this is the case, AI that replicates our functional organization would be a much better candidate for consciousness than simple animals that share our biology."
- "There is little doubt that some aspect of our biology is necessary for consciousness in us, and if necessary in us, might be necessary, period. While this is a modest conclusion, it is important if true." "In practice, we will inevitably be most sure of the consciousness of systems that are like us both computationally and subcomputationally, and less sure when one or the other is absent."
- **Suffering without consciousness:** "even if consciousness does require meat, that leaves open the possibility of AI suffering, because suffering may not require consciousness. Indeed, the stifling of AI agency might itself be a kind of suffering [Long et al. 2024]. I doubt that current AI models have anything that could be called agency, but future AI systems could conceivably have agency without consciousness." (Unconscious agency in humans: the dorsal visual stream as "something like a robot", Clark 2001.)
- "If meat is directly responsible for consciousness, we do not know what biological feature is responsible or why. A breakthrough will be needed."

**Outstanding questions (all five).** How can we tell whether roles or realizers are responsible? "Do AI systems that have not been designed to mimic us (unlike current large language models) show markers of consciousness? Do naturally evolved creatures whose ecological niche is so different from ours that their functional roles bear little resemblance to our own evince markers of consciousness?" If realizers matter, what makes the difference: "Perhaps a sophisticated organic creature could be designed or simulated that had only electrical synapses. Would it show markers of consciousness?" Would finding that meat is necessary bring us closer to the hard problem? Prospects for "an artificial system that has electrochemical properties like ours"? If illusionism is true, does the role/realizer question survive? And if robots "look, act, and talk like humans", does that push us toward "a disjunctive theory of consciousness, one that specifies that consciousness can either be grounded in functional role or in biology"?

## Spot checks [R]

- **Cheng 2023:** "only one well-controlled study on classical conditioning in sea anemones provided firm evidence, and no studies firmly supported operant conditioning in Cnidaria." Matches. Block's claim that the box jellyfish is capable of operant conditioning has no citation in the paper (ref 24 is only for its date).
- **Burkhardt et al. 2023:** "neurons in the subepithelial nerve net have a continuous plasma membrane that forms a syncytium." Matches; "entirely electrical" is Block's gloss on a syncytium.
- **Schultz et al. 2023:** the quoted phrase is in the abstract ("rare and irreversible chromosome fusion-and-mixing events"). One imprecision: Block says the paper showed comb jelly chromosomes "were radically different from those of other animals". The abstract's finding is the other way round: ctenophores keep the *ancestral* arrangement shared with single-celled relatives, and it is sponges, cnidarians and bilaterians that share derived rearrangements. Nothing in his argument depends on it.

## How it bears on the *Nature* comment and essay 019 [M]

1. **It is the most hedged of the three authors' sources.** The comment cites Block 2026 for one candidate substrate (electrochemical synapses) in its reason 3 and concludes that "the balance of theory and evidence strongly suggests" silicon AI is unlikely to be conscious. Block's own paper calls the role and realizer hypotheses "equally plausible", calls its conclusion "modest", gives "only weak reason" about what the electrochemical advantage was, and says "a breakthrough will be needed". It also states the opposite possibility, in which AI with our functional organization "would be a much better candidate" than simple animals. Session 19 found the same gap between Seth's BBS paper ("may well be wrong ... altogether") and the comment; Block's paper widens it.
2. **IIT is meat-neutral in Block's own classification.** The comment groups IIT with biological naturalism under "theories of consciousness" that don't treat consciousness as computation. That's consistent with Box 1 (IIT is not computational functionalism), but Box 1 also says IIT is indifferent to meat: its "no" for current AI is about how the chips are wired, so a differently wired machine could pass. Of the comment's two substrate verdicts, only one is about biology.
3. **The AlphaFold control is Block's first outstanding question.** "Do AI systems that have not been designed to mimic us (unlike current large language models) show markers of consciousness?" Essay 019 answered it on paper for AlphaFold with Butlin et al.'s indicators; session 20 runs one marker, Gurnee et al.'s workspace signatures, on a GPT-2-small model trained on proteins, next to the same design trained on English and an untrained copy ([notes](jlens-compare-2026.md)). Result: the protein model shows every structural signature the English one does, several more clearly, and the untrained copy none, so at this scale the answer to Block's question, for this marker, is yes. Block's framing also says what that test can and can't show: it measures roles, so on the meat hypothesis its result doesn't matter, and on the role hypothesis it matters exactly as much for the protein model as for the chatbot.
4. **The face-and-behavior prediction is the comment's diagnosis.** "People's beliefs about consciousness will likely depend primarily on factors such as whether the systems in question have a face and resemble us behaviorally" is the AlphaFold point made as a prediction about people.
5. **Suffering without consciousness.** The comment ends: "Silicon, digital AI systems should be considered objects, not subjects", and welfare protection "is not just mistaken". Block's paper, cited in the same article, leaves open AI suffering *even if* meat is necessary for consciousness, through agency, and cites the same report (Long et al. 2024) the comment cites for the welfare calls it rejects. For current systems the two are compatible, since Block doubts current models have agency. As a general default ("objects, not subjects") the comment goes further than one of its authors' own papers: on Block's view, whether a system can be harmed is not settled by whether it is conscious. That is the agency route to welfare that essays 006, 011 and 015 took seriously, and the one that makes self-funding agents (essay 015) the practical question.
6. **The indicator method assumes what Block questions.** Butlin et al.'s first assumption is his target. Scoring AlphaFold with their list (essay 019) is therefore a comparison *within* computational functionalism; Block's point is that this whole axis may be the wrong one. The notebook's table already has both axes (functional rows; substrate rows that look at the hardware and the organism), and Block's "competitors" thesis is what the table looks like read row by row: the functional rows rank AI above insects, the substrate rows rank insects above AI.
