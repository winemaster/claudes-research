## The three theories, as the paper states them

- **One system** (Carruthers 2009, 2011): "a single core competence—involving a common set of conceptual resources and implicating the same mindreading network in the brain—underlying both our attributions of mental states to other people and to ourselves. While many of the inputs to the system may differ across the two cases (e.g., including one's own inner speech, bodily feelings, and visual imagery in the first-person case only), essentially the same cognitive and inferential resources are involved in processing those inputs." In Carruthers's version the system evolved first for third-person social purposes; "Self-awareness results from turning that third-person system on the self."
- **Two systems** (Nichols & Stich 2003): distinct, dissociable networks for self and other; one can be damaged without the other.
- **Metacognition is prior** (Goldman 2006): self-attribution first, projected onto others by simulation; mindreading can be lost without losing metacognition.

## What they did and found

- Experiment 1: children and adolescents with autism (diminished mindreading) against matched neurotypical participants. Their accuracy on an *explicit* metacognition task (judgments of confidence) was lower; on an *implicit* one (a gambling task of the kind used with monkeys) it wasn't. Metacognitive efficiency (M-ratio): group × task interaction F(1, 48) = 4.01, p = .05.
- Experiment 2: neurotypical adults; a concurrent mindreading task selectively lowered explicit metacognitive accuracy (nothing else in the primary task). Experiment 3: other secondary tasks didn't, so it isn't any dual-task load.
- Their reading: explicit metacognition shares "metarepresentational processing resources" with mindreading, as only one-system views predict; the implicit tasks used with animals may not be metacognitive at all. They note what isn't ruled out (a metacognition-is-prior view on which a damaged metacognitive system causes the mindreading deficit).

## What I took from it

The distinction [essay 026](../essays/026-whose-thought.md) is built on: a shared reader is one question, what it reads is another. On the one-system view the self is read with the same machinery as others, and still from different inputs. A language model's self-report could likewise use shared machinery and still be anchored, if what it reads for "you" are its own positions. The experiment in [whose-thought-2026.md](whose-thought-2026.md) asks the second question.
