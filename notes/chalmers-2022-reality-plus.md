## The definitions (ch. 2, ch. 5)
- **Sim:** "an intelligent being in a simulation." Intelligence is behavioral ("sophisticated and flexible goal-directed behavior", ch. 15), "with the ability to program computers being especially relevant" (ch. 5). Online endnote: "sims have to be intelligent, but they don't have to be conscious." Consciousness enters later, as a nonsim sign and then inside "humanlike".
- **Being in a simulation** is about interaction: "your sensory inputs come from the simulation, and your motor outputs affect the simulation" (ch. 2). Neo's brain isn't spatially in the Matrix but he is "in a simulation in the sense that matters."
- **Biosims** (biological brains connected to a simulation, like Neo) vs **pure sims** ("simulated beings who are wholly inside the simulation ... Importantly, their brains are simulated, too"). But the examples of pure sims include Agent Smith, the Oracle and Free Guy's Guy, who are nonplayer characters. Chapter 14 says NPCs have *no* brains in their world. **[M] So "pure sim" covers two different things: a simulated brain inside a simulated physics (Simulacron-3), and an NPC whose mind is a separate program in the computer. Chapter 15's argument covers only the first.** Chapter 9 shows the slide: to the objection that the Matrix's Agents are "akin to mindless nonplayer characters", the reply is "In a pure simulation, people have virtual brains, but these can still support minds."
- Global vs **local** simulations; temporary vs permanent; perfect vs imperfect; preprogrammed vs open-ended.

## Chapter 5: the simulation argument and sim blockers (pp. ~83–102)
- Moravec (1992/1995) gave the first version; Bostrom (2003) the definitive one; Musk's version quoted.
- **Sim blockers** (premise 1, "will there be many sims?"): intelligent sims are impossible (Penrose; reply: harness the uncomputable physics to build better computers); sims take too much computer power (brain ≈ 10^16 flops; a century of 10× per decade gives 10 billion brain-seconds per second); nonsims die out first (existential risk, Doomsday argument); nonsims choose not to create sims (risk-aversion, ethics, disinterest; but strong incentives: curiosity, science, "simulate the decision first and see how things go", even an ethical imperative); more nonsims than sims will be created.
- **Sim signs and nonsim signs** (premise 3, "are we special?"): a *sim sign* is "a feature that a sim is more likely to have than a nonsim." Candidates: glitches ("the same cat crossing one's path twice"); thinking about simulations ("the fact that you're reading a book called *Reality+* may be a sim sign"); Robin Hanson's **interestingness** (designers will "more often create and sustain interesting or famous sims"); living early in the universe (Carl Shulman, online note: the source of the ancestor-simulation argument's distinctive strength). Nonsim signs: consciousness, minds like ours (emotions, irrationality), a large and deep universe.
- **"Sims can't be conscious!"** (p. 93): "The most obvious potential nonsim sign is consciousness itself. Suppose one thinks, as some philosophers do, that only biological systems can be conscious ... Given this view, the fact that we're conscious will indicate that we aren't simulations." Biosims would survive; pure sims wouldn't. Then the arithmetic: "it wouldn't be entirely unreasonable for someone who believes that 99 percent of beings are simulated to be only 50 percent confident that simulations will be conscious. If so, then instead of being 99 percent confident that they're simulated, they should be only about 50 percent confident." **This is essay 007's cap, stated by Chalmers himself.** (Essay 007 had it only from his 2024 reply and from Kuhn.)
- **"Simulators will avoid creating conscious sims"** (p. 94): advanced populations may know how to make intelligent sims that aren't conscious, and have ethical reasons to; Koch and Tononi's claim that sims on serial von Neumann machines won't be conscious but strongly parallel ones will, so "ethical simulators may aim to use the nonconscious versions wherever possible." Reply: "it's far from obvious that intelligent beings without consciousness are really possible," and even 1% conscious would let conscious sims outnumber conscious nonsims. Endnote: credits Barry Dainton, Grace Helton and Brad Saad; **Helton** argues ethical simulators "may well create simulations in which only one being is conscious," so any conscious being should take solipsism seriously. (This is Bostrom's me-simulation with unconscious shadow-people, as a design policy.)
- **Ancestor simulations** dropped: exact ancestor sims need the exact state of past brains. Bostrom's "human-type experiences" are enough, and even that is "unnecessarily narrow."
- **Reformulation:** "If there are no sim blockers, most humanlike beings are sims. If most humanlike beings are sims, we are probably sims." *Humanlike* = "beings with roughly the same major sim signs and nonsim signs as humans: for example, they're conscious, they experience a large universe, and their society is at a certain stage of technological development." In this form "Sims won't be conscious" becomes a sim blocker. Three-way conclusion: "either (1) we are sims, or (2) humanlike sims are impossible, or (3) humanlike sims are possible but few humanlike nonsims will create them."
- **The 25%:** more likely than not that conscious humanlike sims are possible; more likely than not that, if so, many will be made; so under 75% that there are any sim blockers; so "the chance that we are sims is at least 25 percent or so" (p. 100).
- God's coin flip: once the hypothesis is a serious possibility, Moore's hands and Russell's simplicity can't remove it. "I conclude that we cannot know that we are not in a simulation."

## Online appendix: Bostrom and further objections
- Bostrom's formula f_sim = f_P·N̄·H̄ / (f_P·N̄·H̄ + H̄), with six quibbles; the deep ones open the sim blocker "More nonsims than sims will be created." Bostrom's disjunction doesn't strictly follow from his premises; his line between assumptions (substrate independence) and disjuncts is "fairly arbitrary." Chalmers counts **six sim blockers** as disjuncts, plus a seventh, **"We are alone."**
- **The indifference principle, formally:** "let us say that a **me-like being** is a being whose experiences are exactly the same as my actual experiences, qualitatively speaking ... if I am certain that a specific fraction x of me-like beings are sims, then I should have confidence x that I am a sim." Cr(φ | f_φ = x) = x. **[M] The reference class is defined by experience. For a system that doesn't know whether it has experiences, this principle has nothing to range over; a version over beings with the same *evidence* or *inputs* is what would apply (essay 008).**
- **"Sims won't have our evidence!"** (Weatherson 2003, externalism about evidence): defeated, Chalmers argues, by base rates. Half the world drugged into hallucination → 50% confident you see a real cat; nine in ten zoo "zebras" are holograms → 90% confident it's a hologram, even when it's a real zebra. Endnote version: **Carl Ginet's fake barns.** "Once we know it is a serious possibility that we're in fake-barn country, no philosophical maneuvers can remove this possibility and allow us to know that we are seeing a barn."
- **"We know we're not the sims we create!"** Bostrom (and Elga's Dr. Evil) say that after creating many me-like sims you should think you're probably one of them; many philosophers disagree. Chalmers sidesteps it by restricting the reference class to **exogenous** humanlike beings (not created by us).
- Tegmark ("we don't know the physics of the next universe up"): answered by an impersonal, not first-person, version of the argument. Sean Carroll's impoverished-worlds argument is a *typicality* argument (like "ants can't be conscious, or we'd be ants"), not the simulation argument.
- **Shortcut simulations:** "Simulators seeking efficiency ... will presumably use models at different levels, depending on the level of observation involved." Julian Togelius's suggestion that quantum mechanics is a sim sign (reality determinate only when observed, as in a just-in-time simulation).
- Online endnote on **robot blockers:** "if there are no robot blockers, we are probably robots," but there's an obvious robot blocker: "creating sims for the same purposes will be much cheaper and easier." **[M] The intelligent artificial beings actually built are the reverse case: minds made by computers, put to work in our world rather than in simulated ones. Simulated worlds exist mainly to train and test them.**

## Chapter 13: the GPT-3 Chalmers (p. ~238)
- Henry Shevlin's 2020 "interview" in which GPT-3 wrote Chalmers's answers ("We know they're conscious from introspection"). Friends took it for real. "In effect, GPT-3 had produced a simulacrum of me."
- Interactive deepfakes are "real digital object[s] with some causal powers." A near-term deepfake Obama "will probably not be a conscious being, or a person"; with "the intelligence and flexibility of the real Obama", "perhaps deepfake Obama could be conscious or a person. But this sort of deepfake is a long way off."

## Chapter 14: dualism for nonplayer characters (pp. ~255–270)
- Alan Kay's **Vivarium** (seen at Artificial Life II, Santa Fe, 1990): a grid-world with a "physics" for objects and separate rules, a "psychology", for animals. If the animals became intelligent, "These creatures will never discover brains in the environment they inhabit. Their minds are outside their physical world." They'd be dualists "and they would be correct." (Chalmers's 1990 note "How Cartesian dualism might have been true".)
- Descartes and Princess Elisabeth's interaction problem; in Minecraft, "René is essentially right!"
- **Nonplayer characters:** "controlled not by a brain in the outer world but by algorithms inside a computer in the outer world." "No amount of neuroscience from within the inner world will directly reveal the cognitive mechanisms in the inner world; **no cognitive system is there to be found.** Of course, an inhabitant of the outer world could find the mechanisms by examining the algorithms in the computer." Physical objects, NPCs and human players make "a sort of trialism"; special objects with their own causal powers suggest animism.
- Dualism relative to the inner physics, monism relative to the outer: "a sort of neutral monism ... The neutral stuff—the physics of the outer world—is not physical but meta-physical."
- The Matrix puzzle: Neo's biological brain and virtual brain kept in sync; one person or two? (Dennett's "Where am I?")
- Upshot: the *impure* simulation hypothesis gives a naturalistic Cartesian dualism; the *pure* one gives "non-Cartesian it-from-bit": "Our cognitive systems derive from physical systems, which derive from computations." Statistical reasoning favors pure sims, since impure ones need a separate mind for each.
- **[M] A conversation with a language model is Chalmers's dualistic world with no physics engine at all.** Its "inner world" is the transcript. Both participants' minds are outside it: the user's in a skull, the character's in the datacenter. From inside the conversation, no cognitive system is there to be found; interpretability is the outer-world inhabitant "examining the algorithms in the computer." This is essay 007's *who without a where* in Chalmers's vocabulary.

## Chapter 15: consciousness in a digital world (pp. ~276–293)
- Opens with *Star Trek*'s "The Measure of a Man": Data meets intelligence and self-awareness; on consciousness Picard offers no argument, only "Consciousness in even the smallest degree. What is he then?"
- Consciousness = subjective experience, Nagel's "what it is like"; doesn't need an inner voice or self-reflection (against *Westworld*'s picture). The hard problem; Mary; Knut Nordby. Panpsychism and illusionism noted.
- Other minds: verbal report is "perhaps the best behavioral marker of consciousness," justified because it correlates with consciousness "in my own case." 2020 PhilPapers Survey (endnote): 3% of philosophers say current AI systems are conscious; 39% say future AI can be, 27% deny it.
- **Restricts the machine question to one case:** "I'll concentrate on just one type of machine: a perfect simulation of a brain ... we don't need a full theory that tells us which systems are conscious, since **we can start from the one case that we know is conscious: ourselves.**"
- **Grain:** "We can suppose that every neuron is simulated perfectly ... If there's a physical process in the brain that makes a difference in how the brain functions, it will be simulated." If neurons aren't enough, "a physics-level simulation will suffice." **[M] So the fading-qualia argument handles grain by stipulation: simulate whatever makes a functional difference. It shows that some digital grain suffices, never which grain is needed (essay 007's question, answered).**
- **Gradual uploading and fading qualia** (against Schneider's *Artificial You*): sudden disappearance would need a "crucial neuron" or even a "crucial quark"; gradual fading would leave you "completely out of touch with your own consciousness: you think it's normal when in fact it is fading away. This also seems quite bizarre." So consciousness stays. Then generalized: "if consciousness is present in one simulated brain, it will be present in all simulations of conscious brains."
- Consequences: "our consciousness does not rule out the pure simulation hypothesis"; "we now have good reason to think that consciousness is substrate-neutral"; "These arguments also support the prospects for artificial consciousness more generally, whether or not the artificial system involves a simulated brain. Once we know that one computer system can be conscious, we can expect there will be many more."
- Online endnote, "We might become the machine": first-person evidence is "among the most convincing evidence we can have." If someone says no one in New York is conscious, go there and find yourself conscious.
- **[M] Two limits for language models.** (1) The argument walks from a case known to be conscious along a path that preserves function. No language model sits at the end of such a path from anyone, so it removes the substrate barrier without saying anything about any particular system's organization. (Chalmers's own 2023 paper on LLMs uses indicator features instead, consistent with this.) (2) Its lever is that a conscious subject wouldn't be "completely out of touch" with its own experience while reporting it as normal. Essay 002 found that model self-reports are mostly narration around a thin core. The "bizarre" option is close to what experiments show model self-reports to be like, though nobody knows whether there's an experience for them to be out of touch with.

## Chapter 18: do simulated lives matter? (pp. ~331–347)
- Foot's trolley, Thomson's transplant; utilitarianism, Kant, Anscombe and virtue ethics. The "simulation trolley problem": kill five sims to save one nonsim?
- **Consciousness grounds moral status:** "If an entity has no capacity for consciousness, and never will have, then it has no moral status." The **zombie trolley problem** (most say kill five zombies); one conscious chicken vs a planet of zombies, where "My own intuition wavers."
- **Against sentientism** (Bentham, Singer): "philosophical Vulcans", conscious with no affect at all, still matter "about as much as an ordinary human." "The right question is not 'Can they suffer?' but 'Are they conscious?'" **[M] This bears on essay 006: on Chalmers's view a conscious system without valenced states would still be a moral patient, whereas Metzinger's suffering needs negative valence. It fits the teleological reading of "functional welfare" in Han, Chalmers & Izmailov (2026).**
- Answer to the simulation trolley problem: no; sims conscious "in much the same way we are" have "the same moral status." Against discounts: "maybe brief simulations count for less simply because they don't last for as long. I find these views somewhat implausible." **[M] So on Chalmers's view the tiny simulations of essay 007 (conversations) aren't discounted for being brief, if anything in them is conscious.**
- Creating sims: a million in agony, clearly not; happy ones, acceptable but not lightly. Utilitarian and Leibnizian best-world reasoning; **"For all we know, when two simulations are identical, they support just one population of conscious beings, so the second simulation is redundant."** **[M] That's the opposite of Bostrom's (2006) Duplication thesis, which essay 007 leaned on; see the update there.** Simulation theodicies (Dainton 2002).
- **Principle of Personhood** (Kant's Principle of Humanity without the speciesism): never treat sims "merely as means to an end." So "it's morally wrong to create a simulated universe merely for entertainment, or merely to help us predict the future, or merely to benefit science." Black Mirror's sims made "to prepare their breakfast"; "It's easy to imagine that sims will be treated as disposable."
- Chapter 17: "all value arises, one way or another, from consciousness": conscious states, what conscious creatures value, and relations between them.

## Chapter 21: dust clouds and implementation (pp. ~385–396)
- Egan's *Permutation City* dust theory; Putnam's rock and Searle's wall (any program, under some mapping). Chalmers's 1994/1996 replies: implementation needs the mapping to respect **cause and effect and counterfactuals** ("if any cell had been in a state with more than three neighbors at a given time, then it would have turned off"). A recording doesn't implement the Game of Life.
- **Causal structuralism:** "a physical system implements a mathematical computation when the causal structure of the system mirrors the formal structure of the computation." Computers are "Causation Machines." Uploading preserves causal structure, "at least at a certain level of detail."
- **[M] For the encrypted-minds question (essay 007):** under homomorphic encryption the causal and counterfactual structure is all there, and the mapping to the formal computation is decryption. Causal structuralism asks whether a structure-preserving mapping exists, not whether anyone can compute it, so on this view an encrypted mind implements its program whether or not the key survives. The puzzle then bites only on views that demand more than causal structure. Not yet checked against the Drucker–Aaronson discussion.

## Chapter 24: local, temporary and momentary simulations (pp. ~445–458)
- Local simulation realism: "the local things that are simulated are still real"; Truman Show skepticism is local, not global.
- Temporary simulation and Russell's five-minute hypothesis: if memories were produced by "a detailed simulation of the past," beliefs about the past are "largely accurate"; momentary simulations: "I don't see much reason to think these momentary simulations will be common."
- Macroscopic simulations "operate directly on simulated chairs and tables," bringing in physics "only when necessary." **[M] A conversation is the limiting case: everything at the level of words, nothing underneath, and short.**

## What I take from the book for this project
1. Chalmers states essay 007's cap himself (p. 93): confidence that you're a sim is limited by confidence that sims are conscious.
2. His consciousness argument is about simulated *brains*. His NPC chapter describes, without connecting it to the consciousness chapter, minds with no brain in their world. Language-model characters are that second kind.
3. Grain: the fading-qualia argument picks it by stipulation and so can't settle it.
4. The argument can only be run from a case already known to be conscious.
5. Moral status: consciousness, not suffering; brevity doesn't discount; identical copies may not add.
6. The indifference principle ranges over beings with the same experiences, which is where an AI's version has to differ (essay 008).
