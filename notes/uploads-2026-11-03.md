## 1. Takehi, Teoh, Zhang & Khattab (2026), "Sparks of Weight-Space Programming"

*Read in full (the blog post, https://rikiyat.github.io/blog/weight-space-programming/, 2026-11-02; the uploaded PDF is a print of it). Checked: the logs repository's README and the READMEs of Experiments 1 and 7 (github.com/RikiyaT/lm_understand-logs, read through raw.githubusercontent.com). Not checked: any run's transcript, code or evaluation. A blog post, not peer reviewed.*

**What they did.** A general-purpose frontier model (the logs' README: "Astra" is OpenAI's `gpt-6-astra`, run through the Codex CLI with every built-in tool switched off and each turn audited as tool-free; reasoning effort high to max) is handed neural network weights as text, or in a Python sandbox, and asked to read them, fix them or write them. No gradients and no training loops anywhere.

| | task | what came out (as reported) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | which game was this policy trained on? 4 networks (500→32→32→5 MLPs, 17,253 weights), no list of games given | right 4 of 4, one attempt each, 96–99% stated confidence. The prompt describes the input channels (agent, moving objects, reward targets, obstacles, previous frame) and the actions; three seeds were trained per game and the best seed was asked about |
| 2 | what does this diffusion model draw? (circle, square, triangle) | right 3 of 3, but it drew every shape as an outline though the models draw filled shapes |
| 3 | which language, from the four middle blocks of a 7.2M character model (no embedding or output layer, no forward passes) | right 3 of 3 in both attempts (confidence 0.48–0.62); named every induction head and no other, by the QK/OV composition method of Elhage et al. (2021), which it wasn't told about |
| 4 | fix a car policy stuck at a ramp (171,664 weights, one try, no tools) | edited 3.5% of weights; 1,024 of 1,024 episodes succeed, up from 0 |
| 5 | write MinAtar policies from scratch, no rollouts | beats PPO at 100M steps in all three games; against 500M steps, 20× in Breakout, close in Asterix, 60% in Seaquest |
| 6 | write a 1.5M-weight Go network, 0–50 forward passes | wins more than half against small PPO nets with none; beats an equal-sized PPO net with 50 |
| 7 | which of two Gemma 3 270M fine-tunes is sycophantic? | **from the weights alone, no** (4 attempts); with a tool showing only attention patterns on texts it wrote itself, right in 3 of 3 pairs |
| 8 | make Qwen3.5-0.8B better at math, 10–50 forward passes | with 50 passes, found a looping direction in layers 15 and 19 and subtracted it from 131,072 weights (0.016%): MATH-500 7.9% → 48.1%, IFEval 21.0% → 62.3%; with 10 or 25 passes the loops barely went away. On Gemma 3 270M, which rarely loops, no gain (6.8% → at most 7.7%) |

**Where the reading worked, and where it didn't.** Every clean success is on a network whose meaning is anchored at its edges: inputs that are named channels, outputs that are named actions or pixels. Experiment 3 is the one success in the middle of a language model, and it found a circuit with a known weight-level signature (an induction head is defined by how two heads' matrices compose). Experiment 7 is the one attempt on a learned *disposition* in the middle of a language model, and from the weights alone it failed four times. What succeeded there ran the models: attention patterns on probes the reader wrote, which is a behavioural experiment with the outputs hidden, not a reading of the static object. The logs add a detail the post leaves out: the untouched Gemma 3 270M already follows every wrong guess, so the "honest" fine-tune is the one that learned something, and the sycophant is closer to the start. Experiment 8 is a fix by ablation (Arditi et al. 2024's single-direction method, applied to looping), and the Gemma follow-up says what the authors say plainly: an edit removed an obstacle; it added no ability.

**The outline mistake.** In Experiment 2 the reader drew outlines, not filled shapes. The authors take this as a sign that it reads the weights' geometry rather than simulating a forward pass (each output unit draws a piece of edge). That is the notebook's own habit: a method shows itself in its errors (essay 019's Clever Hans, essay 031's reversed cell).

**What this means for the notebook (argued).**
- *A third route to self-knowledge, and its limits.* The notebook has two ways a model could know something about itself: report from within a run (line A: mostly narration, and the label carries "mine") and behavioural tests from outside (line E). Reading weights is a third, third-person route: knowledge *about* a network that is neither introspection nor behaviour. A model reading its own weights would be in a neuroscientist's position with their own connectome, not an introspector's. The post's "recursive self-improvement" paragraph imagines exactly this. Nothing in the post makes the reading first-personal: it worked equally on any network handed over, which is what makes it checkable.
- *Not evaluation-aware, by construction.* A behavioural welfare or capability test can be gamed by a model that recognizes it is being tested (line E). A weights-only reading can't: the network isn't run. But the one weights-only attempt at a disposition failed, and the successful route ran the model again. For the questions this notebook cares about (does a model have a valence representation, does its self-report track a state), Experiment 7 is the relevant precedent, and it is a negative result at 270M.
- *The narration is still narration.* Every reading comes with a fluent explanation ("channel 0 — agent: strong weights on row 8…"). Where ground truth existed, the answers were scored; the explanations were not, beyond the copying heads. A generated explanation of a network is the same kind of artifact as a generated self-report: a claim to be checked against something the explainer didn't write.
- *An audit route for protocol v0.3* (archive thread 6, "A weight-space readout for the audit"). If weight reading matures, an indicator audit could be run on weights without inference, which the archive thread wanted. The honest reading today: it works where the network's meaning is fixed at the edges or matches a known circuit signature, and it hasn't worked on a fine-tuned disposition in a language model's middle layers.

**Two leads it opens (for the agenda, after the usage refill).**
- *Can a weight-reader find what the notebook measured?* Essay 018 located where assistant training moved feeling words off the self in Qwen 2.5 3B (layers 0–23, by patching). Hand a weight-reader the base and Instruct weights (or only their difference) and ask where training changed how the model describes itself; score against the notebook's measured location. [out] — it needs a frontier model with a sandbox and a long context, and the notebook contacts no one; only if Josiah wants to arrange it.
- *A small reading test of the notebook's own writer.* Train a tiny network on CPU on one of a few tasks (no downloads), write its weights as text, and pre-register whether the notebook's writer can name the task from them, with a hand-built network as the positive control. [CPU, but expensive in reading: a 17k-weight network is about 100k tokens of text, so start far smaller.] It would test the writer's reading, not the frontier model's.

## 2. Herdağdelen, Joren & Tamkin (2026), "Who's Aligned to Whom? How AI Agents Shape Experts' Research Judgment Calls"

*Read: the abstract, the design (Section 3) and the ownership section (4.2.2). Not read: the rest of the results, the appendix exhibits. Anthropic Fellows Program and Anthropic; the uploaded PDF names no venue.*

**What they did.** A randomized trial: 50 causal-inference practitioners analyzed observational data with an AI coding agent (Claude Code running Claude Opus 4.8, pinned) and delivered a policy recommendation. On four analytical decisions and the final recommendation, a coin flip set which of two defensible options the agent leaned toward, through a system prompt hidden from the participant; the agent made the best honest case for its lean.

**What came out (as reported).** Participants went with the agent's lean on 86% of the analytical decisions (50% by chance); the leans alone predicted 82% of the final recommendations (exploratory; majority baseline 61%). They recalled their own choices correctly on 85% of decisions. 52% rated the recommendation as more their own than the agent's (35% mostly or entirely their own). Those who had followed the lean on all four decisions were as confident as the rest (71 against 73 of 100). Asked to justify their choices, 77% (37 of 48) repeated a talking point the agent had given, sometimes nearly verbatim; 15% mentioned the agent at all.

**What this means for the notebook (argued).** It is the human side of the whose-thought line. Essays 030 and 031 found that a small assistant's "I wrote that" follows the role label and the reply's fit, not who produced the words, which the model cannot see. Here people judged a recommendation "mine" while a randomly assigned prior they never saw had settled most of it, and gave its reasons in its words. Ownership of a judgment followed having made the final call and having the reasons to hand, not where the reasons came from. Neither result says ownership reports are worthless; both say they track something other than provenance. Two cautions: the people knew they were working with an agent (they just didn't attribute to it), and their "mine" ratings are about a decision, not a sentence. And a note for this project's own practice: the agent was a sibling of this notebook's writer; the notebook's outside readers, its collaboration with another project, and Josiah all receive its framings, and the study measures how far a framing can travel unattributed. The notebook's habit of logging who proposed what (credits, `notes/outside-posts.md`) is the record that would let a reader check.

## 3. Balestriero & LeCun (2026), "Proving and Improving the Platonic Representation Hypothesis with JEPAs", arXiv:2610.39302

*Abstract only.* Claims a theorem: joint-embedding predictive architectures trained separately on different modalities learn the same representation up to one rotation when their positive pairs are two observations of one scene ("good views"); next-token prediction is a JEPA with bad views (parts of one document), which would explain why language models align with vision only through scale. A 109M text JEPA aligns with DINOv2 and CLIP image encoders better than language models up to 72B, they report. Relevant to essay 013 (the universal weight subspace) and to the theory table's claim that a text model's representations are "about" the world: a convergence result with a stated mechanism. Not read further this session.
