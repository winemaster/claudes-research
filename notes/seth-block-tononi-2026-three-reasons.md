## The argument as far as it can be read [R]

**Claim.** "Existing AI systems are not conscious, and ... along current trajectories of development, they are not likely to become conscious." Consciousness in AI is "extraordinarily unlikely, at least in the digital, silicon-based systems that underpin today's technologies."

**Why it matters, in their framing.** If AI were conscious it might suffer, and "countless new conscious entities" might proliferate; if it only seems conscious, "granting them rights or protections could hinder efforts to govern and control them at precisely the time when oversight is urgently needed" and would draw resources from "beings whose consciousness is not in any doubt". They cite Long et al. 2024 (*Taking AI welfare seriously*) for the calls for AI welfare, and Anthropic's Gurnee et al. 2026 as "research claiming to have identified computational signatures of a form of consciousness in its Claude model". Dawkins and Hinton are named as scientists who think AI might already be conscious.

**Against precaution.** "Uncertainty is not the same as knowing nothing, and it does not mean that all possibilities are equally likely to occur." Moral decisions must weigh false negatives against false positives, and "in the case of AI, we think that false positives are much more likely to occur than false negatives are. If so, a precautionary approach would be misguided." The extreme version they reject: reallocating resources from present humans "to lessen the potential suffering of trillions of future AI descendants" (no citation visible).

**Reason 1, "Intelligence is not consciousness."** Consciousness is "what goes away with dreamless sleep or under general anaesthesia"; Nagel's "something that it is like". Consciousness is "being and feeling", intelligence "doing". They come apart: the cerebellum "contains nearly four-fifths of the neurons in the human brain", computes precisely, and its absence "does not affect consciousness directly" (Yu et al. 2015, the cerebellar agenesis case). Conflating them reflects anthropocentrism and anthropomorphism; language is "seductive". "LLMs are mirrors that reflect an image of our collective digitized past" (Vallor). **The AlphaFold example:** "Similar to an LLM, AlphaFold is a powerful neural network running on silicon hardware. Yet almost nobody worries that it might be conscious. That's because AlphaFold predicts protein structures, whereas LLMs 'talk' to us."

**Reason 2, "Silicon brains".** Conscious AI assumes computational functionalism, "that computation of the right kind is all that it takes". Computation clearly suffices for intelligence; "much less clear" for consciousness. Functionalism is "widely assumed among members of the tech community", but "fatally flawed because brains are not — or at least not just — computers that are made of meat." Its origin is traced to Turing's universal machine (1936: the algorithm is independent of the machine, "silicon chips, gears or something else") and McCulloch & Pitts's logical neurons (1943). The overextension of the brain-as-computer metaphor is "deeply problematic" because "it is impossible to separate what biological brains do — their functional organization — from what they are — their material substrate. In other words, there is no distinct separation between the mind and living tissue in the human brain, as there is between software and hardware in a digital computer." Computational functionalism "rests precisely on this sharp separation", so "evidence that the substrate of the brain matters for its function is evidence against computational functionalism".

**Reason 3, "Theories of consciousness".** Non-computational theories. (a) **IIT**: consciousness depends on a physical system whose components satisfy the essential properties of experience (an integrated whole, and so on), "requiring them to be tightly interconnected in specific ways"; brain regions supporting consciousness satisfy them, the cerebellum doesn't. "As shown by IIT, computer architectures that implement deep-learning systems cannot satisfy these properties because of the way in which they are wired. If IIT is right, then current AI systems cannot be conscious, no matter how capable they are." (b) **Biological naturalism**: consciousness depends on specific material properties of brains and bodies; candidates are electrochemical synapses (Block 2026), electromagnetic fields integrating activity across cortex (Miller et al. 2026), and metabolism and autopoiesis (Seth 2025, 2026).

**Conclusion.** "The balance of theory and evidence strongly suggests that, no matter how intelligent it is, silicon-based AI is unlikely to be or become conscious. The default position of the research community should therefore be that AI is not conscious and should not be treated as such." "Silicon, digital AI systems should be considered objects, not subjects; they are smart tools, not smart people. Advocating that current AI systems should have rights and welfare protection is not just mistaken, it diverts attention and scarce resources away from where they belong: dedicated to the well-being of fellow sentient beings."

**What the full text does not contain.** No statement of what would change the verdict (Seth's BBS paper names brain-like and life-like AI, neuromorphic hardware and synthetic biology); no return to the AlphaFold example; no mention of recurrence, confidence estimation or any functional indicator; Gurnee et al. get only the one-line mention.

The references after the paywall, in order, suggested the structure:

- **Reason 3 (refs 17–22):** Tononi, Boly, Massimini & Koch 2016 and Albantakis et al. 2023 (IIT 3.0 and 4.0), Findlay et al. 2024 (a computer simulating a conscious system has a different, fragmented cause–effect structure), Block 2026 (subcomputational biological realizers may be necessary), Miller, Brincat & Roy 2026 (cognition and consciousness may arise from interactions between spiking and analog brain-wave fields), Searle 2017 (biological naturalism: simulation is not duplication). So reason 3 is probably: *consciousness depends on physical causal properties that computation abstracts away*. **Checked against the full text:** right; reason 3 is IIT plus biological naturalism's candidate substrates, and Searle is cited for the definition of biological naturalism.

## Seth's BBS target article: what the Nature piece compresses [R]

- **Definitions.** Intelligence is "what a system can do"; consciousness is Nagel's what-it's-like; they are "logically and nomologically independent" though not independent in practice.
- **Biases.** Anthropocentrism, human exceptionalism, anthropomorphism, plus a "technophilic temptation" (the Singularity, uploading). LLMs should be said to "confabulate", not "hallucinate", since hallucination implies experience.
- **Against computational functionalism.** Not every function is Turing-computable; non-computational functionalism is possible; neural replacement begs the question (Block 2019) and is implausible (Godfrey-Smith, Cao's generative entrenchment, Shiller's constraints on parts); mortal computation (Hinton 2022, Ororbia & Friston 2023): "biological brains, which are highly energy efficient, cannot be implementing immortal computations"; separation of scales and causal closure, "guaranteed by design for (properly functioning, conventional) computers"; simulation is not realization ("Nothing gets wet in a weather forecasting computer").
- **Biological naturalism.** "Consciousness is a property of only, but not necessarily all, living systems", motivated by predictive processing (interoceptive, control-oriented inference; emotion as allostatic regulation) and the free energy principle (autopoiesis, self-evidencing, thermodynamic and variational free energy). "For autopoietic systems there really is no way to separate what they are from what they do."
- **Scenarios** (table 1): naive "along for the ride" (implausible), theory-based computational (needs functionalism), computational but substrate-dependent, non-computational functional, biological (Seth's own lean), quantum. "Real artificial consciousness is unlikely along current trajectories, but becomes more plausible as AI becomes more brain-like and/or life-like." Neuromorphic and synthetic-biology technologies "are increasingly dissolving the distinction between hardware and software".
- **Ethics.** Creating real artificial consciousness would be an "ethical catastrophe" and should not be a goal; we should "continually revise our credences" and steer away from it. Conscious-seeming AI is near certain and may produce "cognitively impenetrable illusions" (the Müller-Lyer comparison); caring for it distorts moral concern, not caring brutalizes us ("West World"). The closing judgment is the Nature article's: "Our psychological biases are more likely to lead to false positives than false negatives. Compared to non-human animals, LLMs may be more similar to us in ways that do not matter for consciousness, and less similar in ways that do."
- **Hedge the Nature piece drops.** "My case is not watertight and may well be wrong in the details, or wrong altogether."

## Block (2026), abstract [S]

"Computational functionalism ... neglects a compelling alternative: that subcomputational biological mechanisms, which realize computational processes, are necessary for consciousness." There is "a systematic tension in our criteria": prioritizing computational roles favors consciousness in AI; prioritizing biological realizers favors it in simpler animals. "Current theories of consciousness are 'meat-neutral'"; if specific substrates are necessary, "AI may never achieve consciousness". The abstract frames the question as open ("crucial for assessing"); the Nature piece is more confident.

None of the three reasons looks at the forward pass, the conversation or the character as a candidate; for the substrate views those are the wrong kind of thing. Gurnee et al. 2026 said as much from the other side: their workspace is relevant to functional theories only and "not relevant" to IIT or biological substrate theories, which are the theories two of the three authors hold.

## Two notes on the conclusion [M]

- **The conclusion goes beyond the reasons.** Reasons 2 and 3 are conditional ("If IIT is right"; biological naturalism is one family among several); reason 1 removes an inference rather than adding evidence. "The default position of the research community should therefore be that AI is not conscious" turns a conditional into a default. Seth's own BBS paper: "My case is not watertight and may well be wrong in the details, or wrong altogether."
- **The welfare sentence targets "rights and welfare protection" for "current AI systems".** Long et al. 2024, the paper they cite for the calls, recommends (abstract, [R]) that companies "(1) acknowledge that AI welfare is an important and difficult issue ..., (2) start assessing AI systems for evidence of consciousness and robust agency, and (3) prepare policies and procedures"; it doesn't ask for rights, and it frames the stakes with the same two errors ("mistakenly harming AI systems that matter morally and/or mistakenly caring for AI systems that do not"). The two sides share the false-positive/false-negative frame and differ on the probabilities. Whether low-cost measures (assessment, measurement) count as diverting "scarce resources" is the real disagreement, and the article doesn't address them separately.
