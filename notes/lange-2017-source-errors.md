# Lange, Hollins & Bach (2017): stealing and donating in memory for who did what

**Source:** Lange, N., Hollins, T. J. & Bach, P. (2017). Testing the motor simulation account of source errors for actions in recall. *Frontiers in Psychology* 8:1686. doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01686 (open access, CC BY; read through Europe PMC's full-text XML, PMC5625330, in session 28).

**What was read:** abstract, introduction, the summary of Experiments 1 and 2's error rates, the general discussion's meta-analysis, the reference list. Not read: the methods in detail, Experiment 3, the figures.

**Why it was read:** essay 027's frame. Essay 026 found that a 3B assistant, asked what was on "its" mind in a two-writer story, takes in the other writer's state, and much less the reverse. Psychology has a name and a literature for the human version of the first error, and a finding about the second.

## What it says [R]

- **Cryptomnesia, or unconscious plagiarism** (Brown & Murphy 1989): in "the prototypical study, participants take turns to generate solutions to a problem" (category members, uses for a brick, ways to reduce childhood obesity). "When participants are asked to recall their own solutions, they commonly incorporate solutions generated by their partners, thereby claiming them as their own."
- **Observation inflation** (Lindner, Echterhoff, Davidson & Brand 2010, *Psychological Science*, "Observation inflation: your actions become mine"): people who watched a video of someone performing an action phrase ("Lift the pen") later claimed more often that they had performed it themselves. Lindner et al. attributed it to motor simulation: watching an action engages some of the same neurons as doing it, so the memory of someone else's action carries a motor trace like one's own.
- **The source monitoring framework** (Johnson et al. 1993; [B] Johnson, Hashtroudi & Lindsay, *Psychological Bulletin*) explains both: the source of an item is "not encoded explicitly alongside an item but inferred at retrieval" from the qualitative features that come with it. That account predicts the reverse error too, giving one's own item to the partner, while the motor account predicts a "striking asymmetry": people should readily claim others' actions as their own and rarely the reverse.
- **The reverse error exists, and in one task it is larger.** Hollins, Lange, Berry & Dennis (2016a, *Journal of Memory and Language* 88, "Giving and stealing ideas in memory"), as Lange et al. report it: participants alternated generating solutions to verbal fluency problems; asked to recall their own ideas, they included their partner's; asked to recall their partner's, they reported their own, "at about twice the rate that they reported their partner's ideas in the recall own task".
- **For actions, the two errors are about equal.** Lange et al.'s pairs took turns acting out shapes and letters and recalled the next day either their own actions or their partner's. Experiment 1: 19% of the partner's actions and 17% of their own were reported as source errors; Experiment 2: 15% and 19%. Pooled over three experiments, "both observation inflation and the reverse error ... occur reliably more often than predicted from guessing", and "there is no evidence to suggest that one form of error is more frequent than the other". A motor load during observation didn't reduce the stealing, so they found no evidence for the motor account.

## What it means here [M]

The human literature asked essay 026's question first: is the confusion of "mine" and "theirs" an asymmetry that belongs to the self (motor simulation, which only makes others' actions feel like one's own), or a symmetric failure to tell two sources apart? In people, stealing comes with donating: about equal for actions, donating twice as common for ideas in Hollins et al.'s task. Essay 026's model steals much more than it donates (in text, the self-answer takes Sam's stated thought at 5.9 nats, the answer about Sam takes the assistant's at 1.6). Session 28's experiment asks whether that asymmetry follows the order of writing or the word "you".
