## Bostrom (2003), *Are you living in a computer simulation?*
*Philosophical Quarterly* 53(211), 243–255; first version 2001. **Read in full** (simulation-argument.com/simulation.pdf).
- **The premise (§II):** substrate independence, deliberately weak: "it would suffice for the generation of subjective experiences that the computational processes of a human brain are structurally replicated in suitably fine-grained detail, such as on the level of individual synapses." The intro: "Suppose that these simulated people are conscious (as they would be if the simulations were sufficiently fine-grained and if a certain quite widely accepted position in the philosophy of mind is correct)."
- **What's counted:** "observers with human-type experiences." f_sim = f_P·f_I·N_I / (f_P·f_I·N_I + 1). So the consciousness premise decides what goes into the count at all.
- **Bland indifference (§V):** credence = fraction, but only "provided that you have no information that bears on the question of which of the various minds are simulated and which are implemented biologically." (So it doesn't apply to an AI, which has that information.)
- **The director's tools (§III):** track "the detailed belief-states in all human brains at all times"; fill in detail "on an as-needed basis"; "edit the states of any brains that have become aware of an anomaly"; "skip back a few seconds and rerun." (§VI) false memories for "abridged" parts of a life; **"me-simulations"** with everyone else as "zombies or 'shadow-people' – humans simulated only at a level sufficient for the fully simulated people not to notice anything suspicious." And: "It is not even obvious that it is possible for an entity to behave indistinguishably from a real human and yet lack conscious experience."
- **Disjunct (2), ethical version:** convergence on "an ethical prohibition against running ancestor-simulations because of the suffering that is inflicted on the inhabitants of the simulation"; he doubts it ("it is not clear that creating a human race is immoral").
- **"Naturalistic theogony":** if no one can be sure they're at the basement, everyone has prudential reason to behave ethically; "even the basement civilization may have a reason to behave ethically."
- Conclusion: "apportion one's credence roughly evenly between (1), (2), and (3)."

## Bostrom (2006), *Quantity of experience: brain-duplication and degrees of consciousness*
*Minds and Machines* 16, 185–200. **Read:** abstract and first section.
- **Duplication:** two brains in identical states = two numerically distinct experiences. Argued from ethics: otherwise copying a brain in pain adds no pain, and in an infinite universe we could never cause any experience. Later sections (not read): fractional quantities of experience with unreliable or partly parallel components; implications for fading qualia.
- **My use:** on Duplication, a rerun doesn't undo a run, and discarded samples (regeneration, best-of-n, RL rollouts) all count, if anything counts.

## Chalmers (2024), *Taking the simulation hypothesis seriously*
*Philosophy and Phenomenological Research*, doi:10.1111/phpr.13122. Symposium reply to Godfrey-Smith, Schneider, Schwitzgebel. **Read in full** (consc.net/papers/simserious.pdf).
- **Sim blockers:** "Either there are sim blockers or we are probably sims." Godfrey-Smith's reasons reduce to "(1) Conscious humanlike sims are impossible ... and perhaps (2) Nonsims will not create many conscious humanlike sims." "If we give these denials even 10% credence each, then (assuming independence) this still leaves us with a 1% credence that most humanlike beings are sims."
- **On substrate:** "genuine dynamics is present in computer simulation ... This dynamics will not just represent but replicate the structural dynamics among neurons, at least at a certain level of abstraction." "Around here the real disagreement is which level of abstraction in the dynamics matters for consciousness." "The debate between biological views and functionalist views of consciousness, and the corresponding debate about whether simulations can be conscious, is likely to go on."
- **Pure sims vs biosims:** the Bostrom-style argument supports *pure* sims (brains simulated too), "since pure sims are likely easier to construct in large numbers than biosims."
- **On LLMs as stand-ins:** simulators might use LLMs for distant people, but "these AI systems are themselves high-grade simulations of a sort (even if they simulate behavior more directly than they simulate brains)." Helton's solipsism worry mentioned; "It's far from clear that convincing human behavior without consciousness is possible."
- **Mind control** set aside as distinct from simulation skepticism; "code may be easier to manipulate than brains"; metacognitive skepticism "particularly difficult to defeat."
- **Tiny vs large sims (vs Schwitzgebel):** tiny sims ("simulating me in my room and thinking about philosophy for a few minutes") are "somewhat odd and it's unclear why simulators will be motivated to create them"; headcount favors large sims ("even if there are a million times as many tiny simulations as large simulations, there will be a thousand times as many people in large simulations"). Allows ≥1 in 10 for small sims conditional on being simulated.
- Also: simulation realism (sims' beliefs are mostly true of their own world); against Schneider's wholly illusory worlds.

## Godfrey-Smith (2023), *Simulation scenarios and philosophy*; (2025) *Reply to Chalmers*
Pacific APA symposium on *Reality+* (June 2023 version), and a 3-page reply. **Both read in full** (petergodfreysmith.com; the site blocks curl's default user agent, a browser one works).
- Chalmers's probability: "about 25%" (2023); "at least 25 percent or so" (*Reality+* p. 100, quoted 2025).
- **Substrate independence comes in degrees** and "there's not much of it." His bet (after Bruno van Swinderen): consciousness depends on the interplay of spikes and large-scale sub-threshold oscillations, where "exact timing matters, spatial details matter."
- **Representation vs realization:** "It can't just be a representation ... You need to have a version of the process, some materially different copy of it, in there." "If a lot of substrate independence is unlikely, that makes large numbers of simulated minds less likely."
- Against neuron-replacement arguments: replacement changes micro-behavior, so there's "no reason to believe that experience is unaffected in a slow replacement."
- **Time and memory:** "To seriously suspect you are in a simulation is to suspect that many or all of your memory traces might be cooked up"; *Dark City*; "solipsism of the present moment." 2025: "I'd give 10% probability to the view that 'conscious humanlike sims,' in a broad sense, are possible"; many sims of the needed fidelity "extremely unlikely."

## Schwitzgebel (2022), *Let's hope we don't live in a simulation*; Schwitzgebel & Garza (2015)
- **2022** (The Splintered Mind; LA Times as "Is life a simulation? If so, be very afraid"). **Read in full.** Bostrom ~1 in 3, Chalmers ~25%. Simulators are "basically gamers"; our sims are "games or scientific studies. They run only briefly before being shut down," and are "more easily constructed if they are small, local, and brief, or if they are duplicated off a template." So if we're simulated, expect small, short sims; hope we're not.
- **Schwitzgebel & Garza (2015), *A defense of the rights of artificial intelligences***, *Midwest Studies in Philosophy* 39. **Read:** creators' obligations and design sections. Obligations "similar to those of parent to child or god to creature"; a Sim manager can "directly adjust an AI's individual psychological parameters", "change the past by going back to a save point"; "would also possess the obligations of a god, including probably the obligation to ensure that the AIs contained within don't suffer needlessly." Design: make AIs that provoke reactions matching their real status, and "avoid designing AIs whose moral status is unclear."

## Kleiner & Ludwig (2023), *If consciousness is dynamically relevant, artificial intelligence isn't conscious*
arXiv:2304.05077. **Read:** intro, the section on what theories imply, objections (§8), conclusion. (Seth cites a related Kleiner 2024 on mortal computation, not read.)
- Consciousness is *dynamically relevant* if the system's evolution with it differs from without it. Empirical tests presuppose it; IIT's and GNW's metaphysics imply it (IIT's *math* doesn't).
- Chips are designed and verified to follow a computational theory exactly, so "if consciousness is dynamically relevant, AI systems cannot be conscious." Not an argument against determinism; the point is being "locked into" a formal system. Wiggle room from imperfect verification is random, not systematic. Probabilistic computing leaves only sampling freedom; quantum computing may differ.
- Corollary: "if an AI system states that it is conscious, then this cannot be because it is conscious."
- **My extension [M]:** running a simulation reliably *is* locking a system to a formal model, so the argument applies to any simulated mind on reliable hardware; for anyone who thinks their consciousness affects what they say, it's a sim blocker.

## Kanai & Ma (2026), *Intrinsic computational functionalism and simulated consciousness*
arXiv:2606.15348 (June 2026; Ryota Kanai, Shuqin Ma). **Read:** §3 (simulation/realization/duplication), §5.1, §§7–10; formal sections skimmed.
- Three relations: *simulation* (observer-relative modeling), *intrinsic realization*, *duplication*. "ICF claims that only the realization is the relevant level for consciousness, and mere simulation is not sufficient, and duplication is unnecessary."
- **ICCR** (intrinsic causal-computational realization) preserves physical implementation, intrinsic state individuation, transitions, intervention profiles, the agent-body-world boundary. A movie of a brain fails; a static connectome fails; a boundary lookup table fails unless it realizes the internal mechanisms; a stored-program computer "may or may not."
- Answers to Searle, Putnam triviality, Maudlin's Olympia (idle machinery doesn't count), Block's China brain.
- **vs IIT:** both want intrinsic structure; disagreement is "which grain counts." IIT's exclusion picks one grain; ICF allows "a system can specify consciousness at more than one grain at once," so a simulation could support experience "at its own coarse grain ... whether or not a finer grain does too." The **grain-selection problem** is left open (§9.3).
- Conclusion: "The word 'simulation' does not decide the matter." The question is "which causal-computational organization is realized, at which intrinsic grain, under which interventions and readouts, and with which invariants?"
- **My reading:** the grain problem is the table's "where does it look?" axis; for LLMs, intervention-readout profiles of the simulator's own organization are what interpretability ablations measure.

## Seth (2025), §3.7 and remarks on Bostrom
Preprint osf.io/tz6an_v2, re-read this session for simulation.
- Simulating vs implementing vs realising: "Simulations generally lack the causal powers and intrinsic properties of the things being simulated." "Nothing gets wet in a weather forecasting computer." Footnote 22: the "simulated rainstorm feels wet from inside" reply "presupposes that computation is sufficient for experience." Implementing "at the right level of granularity" would realise consciousness "by definition."
- Intelligence vs consciousness: "Because intelligence is broadly about doing, sufficiently accurate simulations may count as realisations."
- Lists Bostrom's simulation idea among the "posthuman" temptations that bias people toward expecting conscious AI.

## janus (2022), *Simulators*
LessWrong, 2022-09-02. **Read:** framing sections. "Simulator (rule)" vs "simulacra (phenomena)"; "A goal-directed agent which evolves according to physics can be simulated by the physics rule parameterized by an initial state, but the same rule could also propagate agents with different values, or non-agentic phenomena like rocks." Suggests assessing the simulacra-driven systems, not the rule, "for agency, consciousness, and so on."
